# Organization Strategies


## Workbench

I'm really happy with my little workbench. It was very inexpensive and easy to build.

- The bench surface is an IKEA Ingo dining table with a grounded [**Anti-Static Mat**] on top. 
- The shelf brackets are attached to a backboard, which is in turn attached to the window frame. The workbench table isn't attached to anything and can be pulled out to access wiring.
- The shelf has a routed-out groove on the bottom with an LED light strip.
- The overhead light is clamped onto the shelf. It's okay. Not great.
- The backboard has an integrated power strip with USB power.
- Wires from instruments on the shelf are hidden behind the backboard, and behind another board attached to the rear table legs.
- If I run out of room to work I have another table I can roll over, but it doesn't happen very often.

## Tool/Supply Storage

I keep most of my tools and supplies on or near my bench so everything within easy reach.

- Little lucite shelves on the backboard are great for supplies.
- I love stackable trays for holding screws and things. Anything that can possibly roll away goes straight into a tray.
- IKEA SKADIS pegboard is easy to reconfigure and it's easy to design and print custom hangers. I designed the wire spool holders and found most of the others online.

## Large Item Storage

For larger hardware I have four small server racks on wheels that I can roll out when I'm working on the Altair or the teletype or whatever. Each has its own power strip so it's pretty easy to get up and running. They can all fit in my closet in a pinch but I usually have one or two of them out. This was kind of expensive and I'm not 100% convinced it was the best strategy.

## Small Parts Storage

I have found that **flexibility** is the most important design consideration for parts storage. If everything has its own space and everything is sorted and individually labeled it's a nightmare when it comes time to integrate new stuff. I have found that for most parts it works great to put them all labeled bins and then rummage briefly to find what I need. When new parts arrive it's usuallty just a matter of dropping then into bins and I'm done.

### Bins of Bags/Boxes

I use this setup for almost all my parts.

1. Loose parts go into **poly bags**, with labels when it's not obvious what's inside.
1. Bags and assortment boxes go into **bins**.
1. Bins are **labeled**. Categories don't matter. "Capacitors and Screws" is fine. The point is to split things up among bins in a way that uses the space efficiently.
1. Labels are **removable**. When a bin gets ovefull and needs to be split up, make new labels for the new categories.
1. I have a bin labeled **To Be Put Away**, for times when I don't have time to put things away properly. When I'm bored I get that bin out and put everything where it goes.

I have two such setups:

- For things I use a lot, I have A4-size trays from Daiso on an IKEA Kallax shelf next to my workbench.
- For things I use less often, I have lidded tubs (also from Daiso) on a closet shelf.

### Box of Boxes

Connectors are often big and awkward and come in a million types so my bins of bags strategy stopped working … it required two bins  and way too much rummaging. So I recently moved them into a couple box-of-boxes organizers and it's working well so far.

### Drawer Organizer

For mechanical parts (mostly nuts, bolts, and washers) I'm slowly filling up an Agro-Mils drawer organizer, which I probably wouldn't have gotten otherwise but it was free so I took it. The drawers are pretty big so I print subdivider inserts as needed.

I recently added another of these for MCUs, modules, and breakouts because the bins were becoming overfull and hard to rummage through.

### Sorted Storage

I try to avoid sorted storage because maintaining ordering can be a lot of work. But for resistors in particular it's easy and works well for me.

- Resistors go into **poly bags**.
- Bags are **labeled** with restance and tolerance.
- Bags go into a [**business card organizer**](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08XJ4QTSQ), in order.
- Organizer goes into a **cubby**.

I only have an organizer for 1/4W resistors. I use larger values much less frequently so hunting for them in a box isn't much of an issue.

### Indexed Storage

I have many hundreds of ICs at this point and it's impossible to keep track of them without some extra structure. So my strategy for these is:

1. ICs go into **grid divider boxes**. The boxes I use can hold a dozen ICs in each cell. For larger ICs I can take out dividers to make larger cells. Texas is humid so I don't worry about static buildup and haven't had any issues.
2. Boxes are **numbered**. This is like a database key; the numbers have no meaning. I messsed up here when I started so I have several "series" of boxes. This was dumb but isn't easily fixable with the software I'm using.
3. Cells are referenced by sight and are **not labeled**. My boxes are 2x5 cells so I call them 1A, 1B, 2A, 2B, etc.
4. I use [**Partsbox**] to assign parts to locations (i.e., box+cell) and track inventory. It could be done with a spreadsheet but Partsbox automatically links datasheets and does other useful things. But either way it needs to be computerized or indexed with a card catalog because parts aren't sorted.

Additional rules that have worked well:

2. All ICs in a cell have **identical markings**; i.e., otherwise identical chips from different manufacturers go into different bins.
3. If I have too many of a given part to fit in a cell, they go in a **labeled bag** in an **overstock** bin in the closet.
