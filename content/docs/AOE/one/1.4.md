### 1.4.1 Capacitors

A capacitor of \(C\) farads with \(V\) volts across its terminals holds \(Q\) coulombs of charge.

$$ Q=CV $$


We can think of a capacitor as a **frequency-dependent resistor**. Why? Taking the derivative we get

$$ I = C\frac{dV}{dt} $$

which means current is proportional to the rate of change of voltage. At dc our current is zero, and at
higher frequencies more and more current passes through.

1 volt per second across 1 farad supplies 1 amp. What is the rate of change when you apply 1mA to a 1µf capacitor?

$$ \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{I}{C} = \frac{1 \times 10^{-3}}{1 \times 10^{-6}} = 1 \times 10^3 = 1000 V/s$$

By how much will the voltage increase with a 10ms pulse?

$$ .010 s \times 1000 V/s = 10 V $$

How much energy is stored in a capacitor? Energy is the product of charge and *average* voltage, which is half of the final voltage.

$$ E = QV_{avg} = Q \times \frac{1}{2}V = CV \times \frac{1}{2} V = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 $$

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.14
> Take the energy challenge: imagine charging up a capacitor of capacitance C, from \(0V\) to some final 
> voltage \(V_f\). If you do it right, the result won't depend on how you get there, so you don't need to
> assume constant current charging (although you're welcome to do so). At any instant the rate of the flow
> of energy into the capacitor is \(VI\) (joules/s); so you need to integrate \(dU = VIdt\) from start
> to finish. Take it from there.

$$ dU = VIdt = V(C\frac{dv}{dt})dt = VCdV$$

$$ U = \int_{V=0}^{V_f} VCdV 
     = C \int_{V=0}^{V_f} VdV 
    = C \Big[\frac{1}{2}V^2\Big]_0^{V_f} 
    = C\Big[\frac{1}{2}V^2_f - 0\Big] 
    = \frac{1}{2}CV^2_f
$$

So we get the same answer as above.

#### A. Capacitors in parallel and series

Capacitors in parallel are under the same voltage and add capacitances (like resistors in *series*).

$$ C_{total}V = C_1V + C_2V + C_3V+ \cdots  $$
$$ C_{total} = C_1 + C_2 + C_3 + \cdots $$

Capacitors in series have the same inverse sum relationship as resistors in *parallel*.

$$ C_{total} = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3} + \cdots} $$

Or, for exactly two capacitors

$$ C = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2} $$

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.15
> Derive the formula for the capacitance of two capacitors in series. *Hint:* because there is no external connection to the point where the two capacitors are connected together, they must have equal stored charges.

The charges are the same, so both voltages depend on the same \(Q\).

$$ V_1 = \frac{Q}{C_1} \quad\textrm{and}\quad V_2 = \frac{Q}{C_2} \quad\textrm{so}\quad V= Q\Big(\frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} \Big)$$
$$ \textrm{which means}\quad C = \frac{Q}{V}= \frac{Q}{Q\Big(\frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} \Big)} = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}} = \frac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2} $$

The interesting thing here is that there is only one \(Q\). The total charge isn't \(2Q\) because the power supply only pushes \(Q\) coulombs into \(C_1\). \(C_2\) is charged by current flowing out of \(C_1\).

### 1.4.2 RC circuits: V and I versus time

When we're talking about a capacitor discharging, the current comes back the other way so we make it negative. The book doesn't explain this.

$$ C\frac{dV}{dt} = I = -\frac{V}{R} $$

Solving for \(V\) (a differential equation) gives us the voltage across the capacitor as it discharges through a resistor.

$$ V = Ae^{-t/RC} $$

The book doesn't explain what the \(A\) is, but it's the starting voltage. This is an exponentially decaying value. The expression \(RC\) is the **time constant** and is in seconds. After one time constant the voltage decays to

$$ \frac{Ae^{-t/RC}}{A} = \frac{Ae^{-1}}{A} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368 \approx 37\% \ \textrm{of its initial value} $$

After five constants the voltage decays to

$$ e^{-5} \approx 0.0067 \approx 0.7\% \ \textrm{of its initial value} $$
