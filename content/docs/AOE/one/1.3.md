### 1.3.1 Sinusoidal signals

A sinusoidal signal is specified as

$$ V = A\sin(2\pi ft) \quad\textrm{or}\quad  V = A\sin(2\pi ft + \phi) $$

where \(A\) is the amplitude, \(f\) is the frequency in hertz, \(t\) is elapsed time
in seconds, and \(\phi\) is an optional phase offset in radians at \(t=0\). We can also define it in terms of the **angular frequency** \(\omega=2\pi f\) which gives

$$ V = A\sin(\omega t) $$

**A linear circuit driven by a sine wave always outputs a sine wave.** This isn't necessarily the case for other kinds of signals and it's among the reasons this kind of waveform is so useful. We define **frequency response** as the way a circuit alters an input sine wave as a function of frequency.

### 1.3.2 Signal amplitudes and decibels

In addition to amplitude A we also have pea-to-peak amplitude (2A) and RMS amplitude.

$$ V_{rms} = (1/\sqrt{2})A \approx 0.707A $$

Not yet clear why this is important but I'm sure we will come back to it.

#### A. Decibels

Decibels are a logarithmic measure of the ratio two signals. First the ratio of **power**:

$$ dB = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_2}{P_1} $$

It makes sense to be logarithmic because the ratios might be very large or very small, but it's unclear where the \(10\) comes from. Maybe it's just to make the scale more useful?

We also use decibels to measure the ratio of **amplitide**, in which case the values are
scaled by \(20\) rather than \(10\). Not sure why.

$$ dB = 20\log_{10}\frac{A_2}{A_1} $$

There are also scales for **absolute** values, where the basis is some known reference.

- 0dBV amplitude is 1 volt RMS
- 0dBm power is 1 mW at a stated load impedance (50 ohms for RF, 600 ohms for audio); or
- 0dBm amplitudes, 0.22 volts RMS at 50 ohms, and 0.78 volts RMS at 600 ohms

Others exist for various measurements but these are the common ones.

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.12
> Determine the voltage and power ratios for a pair of signals at the following decibel ratios: (a) 3dB, (b) 6dB, (c) 10dB, (d) 20dB.

Solving for the ratio gives us

$$ dB = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_2}{P_1} $$
$$ dB = 10\log_{10}R $$
$$ dB/10 = log_{10}R $$
$$ R = 10^{dB/10} \quad\textrm{and}\quad R = 10^{dB/20} $$

| dB | Voltage Ratio | Power Ratio |
|----|---------------|-------------|
| 3  | 1.412         | 1.995       | 
| 6  | 1.995         | 3.981       |
| 10 | 3.162         | 10          |
| 20 | 10            | 100         |
