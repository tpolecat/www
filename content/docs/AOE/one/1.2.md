This section mostly talks about resistors: combining them in series and parallel, and building/analyzing voltage dividers. A big idea (to me) is that while electronics **seems like** it should be compositional, it turns out that the rules of composition are complicated. Input signals can be distorted if we load them down too much, which we see over and over in the discussion and exercises. We can limit this effect by making \(R_{load} \gg R_{source}\).

### 1.2.1 Voltage and current

- When things are hooked in series, current is constant and voltage add.
- When things are hooked in parallel, voltage is constant and currents add.

Power in watts:

$$P=VI $$

### 1.2.2 Relationship between voltage and current: resistors

#### A. Resistance and resistors

Ohm's law

$$R = V/I \quad\textrm{or}\quad V = IR \quad\textrm{or}\quad I = V / R$$

applies [only] to things like resistors, which are linear in the relationship between \(V\) and \(I\).

#### B. Resistors in series and parallel

Resistors in **series** add

$$ R = R_1 + R_2 $$

and resistors in **parallel** combine in a less intuitive way.

$$ R = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\quad\text{or}\quad R=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}}$$

And in general

$$ R=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \cdots}$$

for any number of resistors in parallel. These formulas are derived from Ohm's law below in exercises 1.3 and 1.4.  It's worth noting that for multiples of the same size resistor in parallel we have

$$ \underbrace{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{R} + \cdots}}_{n\ \textrm{times} } = \frac{R}{n} $$

which is a good shortcut. I also observed that

$$ \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{R_1}{\frac{R_1}{R2} + 1} $$

and because resistor values are positive the denominator here is always greater than 1, so the result will always be less than \(R_1\). Intuitively, a resistor in parallel with an infinite resistance is the same as the resistor on its own, and indeed the limit as \(R_2\to \infty\) is \(R_1\). This same argument applies for \(R_2\), so the total resistance will always be **less than the smaller resistor**. 

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.1
> You have a 5k resistor and a 10k resistor. What is their combined resistance (a) in series and (b) in parallel?

In series we use formula (1.3) and substitute.

$$R = R_1 + R_1 = 5k + 10k = 10k \Omega$$

In parallel we use formula (1.4) and substitute.

$$R = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2} = \frac{5k \cdot 10k}{5k + 10k} = \frac{50M}{15k} = 3.3\bar{3} k\Omega$$

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.2
> If you place a 1 ohm resistor across a 12 volt car battery, how much power will it dissipate?

Using the power formula for resistors we find

$$ P = \frac{V^2}{R} = \frac{12^2}{1} = 144W $$

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.3
> Prove the formulas for series and parallel resistors.

For series circuits we know that voltages add and current is constant.

$$R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{V_1 + V_2}{I} = \frac{IR_1 + IR_2}{I} = R_1 + R_2$$

For parallel circuits we know that currents add and voltage is constant.

$$R = \frac{V}{I_1 + I_2} = \frac{V}{\frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2}} = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}}$$

Multiplying this through gives us the formula for two resistors in parallel.

$$\frac{1}{\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}} = \frac{1}{\frac{R_2}{R_1 R_2} + \frac{R_1}{R_2 R_1}} = \frac{1}{\frac{R_1 + R_2}{R_1 R_2}} = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2} $$

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.4
> Show that several resistors in parallel have resistance
> $$R=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \cdots}$$

As above, for parallel resistors we know that currents add and voltage is constant, so

$$R = \frac{V}{I_1 + I_2 + \cdots} = \frac{V}{\frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \cdots } = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \cdots}$$

#### C. Power in resistance

Using Ohm's law to substitute for \(I\) and \(V\) in \(P=IV\) we get the following laws for power in resistance:

$$P=\frac{V^2}{R}\qquad \textrm{and}\qquad P = I^2R$$

But note that this is only valid for components like resistors that obey Ohm's law.

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.5
> Show that it is not possible to exceed the power rating of a 1/4 watt resistor of resistance greater than 1k, no matter how you connect it, in a circuit operating on a 15 volt battery.

In such a circuit the largest possible voltage across the resistor is 15 volts, so the power dissipation of a 1k resistor would be

$$P = \frac{V^2}{R} = \frac{15^2}{1000} = 0.225W$$

which is less than a quarter watt. Because \(R\) appears in the denominator, any larger value will result in even less power dissipation.

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.6
> New York City requires about \(10^{10}\) watts of electrical power, at 115 volts (this is plausible: 10 million people averaging 1 kilowatt each).
> A heavy power cable might be an inch in diameter. Let's calculate what will happen if we try to supply the power through a cable 1 foot in
> diameter made of pure copper. Its resistance is \(0.05\mu\Omega\) (\(5 \times 10^{-8}\) ohms) per foot. Calculate (a) the power lost 
> per foot from "\(I^2R\) losses,"  (b) the length of cable over which you will lose all \(10^{10}\) watts, and (c) how hot the cable will
> get, if you know the physics involved (\(\sigma = 6 \times 10^{-12}W/K^4 \mathrm{cm}^2\)). If you have done your computations correctly,
> the result should seem preposterous. What is the solution to this puzzle?

What is the current at \(10^{10}\) watts and 115 volts? \(P=VI\) so \(I=P/V\), thus

$$ I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{10^{10}}{115} \approx 86.9 \times 10^6 A $$

Loss per foot is \(I^2R\), which is

$$ I^2R \approx (86.9 \times 10^6)^2 \times (5 \times 10^{-8}) \approx (7.55 \times 10^{15}) \times (5 \times 10^{-8}) \approx 37.75 \times 10^7 W $$

Dividing this into \(10^{10}\) yields the length in feet over which all power is lost.

$$ \frac{10^{10}}{37.75 \times 10^7} \approx \frac{10^3}{37.7} \approx 26.5 \textrm{ft} $$

To get the temperature we need the surface area of the cylinder, which is is \(2\pi(rh+r^2)\). One foot is 30.48 cm, so
\(r = 15.24\) cm and \(h = 26.5 \times 30.48 = 807.72\) cm.

$$ A = 2\pi(rh + r^2) = 2\pi(15.24 \times 807.72 + 15.24^2) $$
$$ = 2\pi \times 12541.9 = 78803\ \textrm{cm}^2 $$

The Stefan-Boltzmann law says \(P = \sigma AT^4\) where \(A\) is the surface area in \(\textrm{cm}^2\) and \(T\) is the temperature in Kelvin.
Solving for \(T\) gives

$$ T = \sqrt[4]{\frac{P}{\sigma A}} = \sqrt[4]{\frac{10^{10}}{(6 \times 10^{-12} \times 78,803)}} $$
$$ = \sqrt[4]{\frac{10^{10}}{4.73 \times 10^{-7}}} $$
$$ = \sqrt[4]{2.1 \times 10^{16}} $$
$$ \approx 12000 K $$

which is more than twice the temprature at the surface of the sun. The solution is to deliver electricity at much higher voltage, lowering
the current but maintaining the same wattage. To illustrate, at 115kV the current is

$$ I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{10^{10}}{115 \times 10^3} = 86.9 \times 10^3 A $$

The loss per foot is

$$ I^2R \approx (86.9 \times 10^3)^2 \times (5 \times 10^{-8}) \approx (7.55 \times 10^6) \times (5 \times 10^{-8}) \approx 377 W $$

Dividing into \(10^{10}\) gives

$$ 10^{10} / 377 \approx 26525299\ \textrm{ft} \approx 5000\ \textrm{miles} $$

### 1.2.3 Voltage dividers

Given a voltage divider with series resistors \(R_1\) and \(R_2\), the ratio of voltage measured in the center to that across both resistors is

$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \quad\textrm{or}\quad V_{out} = V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} $$

assuming no load. We can derive this formula using Ohm's law.

$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = \frac{V_2}{V_1 + V_2} = \frac{IR_2}{IR_1 + IR_2} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}  $$


### 1.2.4 Voltage sources and current sources

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.7
> What will a \(20{,}000\ \Omega/V\) meter read, on its \(1V\) scale, when attached to a \(1V\) source with an
> internal resistance of \(10k\)? What will it read when attached to a \(10k-10k\) voltage divider
> powered by a "stiff" (zero source resistance) \(1V\) source?

A \(20k\ \Omega/V\) meter on its \(1V\) scale appears as a \(20k \times 1 = 20k\Omega\) resistor, which is \(R_2\)
in the divider created with the \(10k\) input resistance. The voltage appearing at the divider is

$$ V_{out} = V_{in} \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = 1 \frac{20k}{30k} = \frac{2}{3} V $$

which is much less than the desired \(1V\). The current across the meter is then

$$ I = \frac{V}{R} \approx \frac{0.666}{20k} \approx 0.0000333 A = 33\mu A $$

Which on a \(50\mu A\) meter is \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the range, representing \(\frac{2}{3}V\) as expected.

When attached to a \(10k-10k\) divider, the \(20k\) resistance is in parallel with the second \(10k\)
resistor in the divider. The parallel resistance is thus

$$ R_p = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{10k \times 20k}{10k + 20k} = \frac{200M}{30K} = 6.66k \Omega $$

Solving the divider we get somewhat less than the hoped-for \(0.5V\).

$$ V_{out} = V_{in} \frac{R_p}{R_1 + R_p} \approx 1 \frac{6.66k}{16.666} \approx 0.4V $$

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.8
> A \(50\mu A\) meter movement has an internal resistance of \(5k\). What shunt resistance is needed to
> convert it to a \(0-1A\) meter? What series resistance will convert it to a \(0-10V\) meter?

To register full-scale on the meter we need \(50\mu A\) across the meter, which acts like a \(5k\)
resistor. The voltage we need here is

$$ V = IR = (50 \times 10^{-6}) \times (5 \times 10^3) = 0.25 V $$

The parallel resistor will see the same voltage and the remaining \(999{,}950 \mu A\) of current.

$$ R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{0.25}{0.999950} \approx 0.25001 \Omega $$

This is a very small value, so the shunt resistor will need to have a high-precision trimmer. 

For the second part we're building a divider. We want the drop across the movement \(R_2\) to be \(0.25V\) (as computed above) when \(V_{in}\) is \(10V\). So we need to drop \(9.75V\) in \(R_1\).

$$ R_1 = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{9.75}{0.000050} = 195k \Omega $$

> [!TIP] Exercise 1.11
> Show that \(R_{load} = R_{source}\) maximizes the power in the load for a given source
> resistance. Note: skip this exercise if you don't know calculus, and take it on faith
> that it is true.

I don't remember much calculus but I can look it up! I remember enough to know that we want to find the derivative of \(P_L\) with respect to \(R_L\) and find the zero(s).

First let's find the current so we can compute power. The current is the same everywhere so it should be easier than trying to do this with voltage.

$$ I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{V_S}{R_S + R_L} $$

From this we get an expression for \(P_L\) that we want to maximize.

$$ P_L = I^2 \times R_L = R_L(\frac{V_S}{R_S + R_L})^2 =  \frac{R_L V_S^2}{(R_S + R_L)^2} $$

We use the **quotient rule** to take get the derivative of \(P_L\) with respect to \(R_L\). The quotient rule says

$$ (\frac{a}{b})' = \frac{a'b - b'a}{b^2} $$

In this case

$$ a = R_L V_S^2 \quad\textrm{and}\quad b = (R_S + R_L)^2 $$

$$ a' = V_S^2 \quad\textrm{and}\quad b' = 2(R_S + R_L) $$

So \(dP_L/dR_L\) is

$$ \frac{dP_L}{dR_L} = \frac{V_s^2 (R_S + R_L)^2 - 2(R_S + R_L)R_L V_S^2}{(R_S + R_L)^4}  $$

Set this to zero to find the maximum.

$$ \frac{V_s^2 (R_S + R_L)^2 - 2(R_S + R_L)R_L V_S^2}{(R_S + R_L)^4} = 0 $$
$$ V_s^2 (R_S + R_L)^2 - 2(R_S + R_L)R_L V_S^2 = 0 $$
$$ V_s^2 (R_S + R_L) \times (R_S + R_L) - 2R_L \times V_S^2 (R_S + R_L) = 0 $$
$$ (R_S + R_L) - 2R_L  = 0 $$
$$ R_S + R_L = 2RL $$
$$ R_S = R_L $$


### 1.2.6 Small-sigal resistance

For some nonlinear components we can talk about dynamic resistance, \(\Delta I/\Delta V\) that tells us
the slope of the \(I-V\) curve at a particular point. This doesn't describe the entire curve, but tells
us how we can expect small deviations from a reference current to affect voltge. This is effectively the
slope of the curve; voltage will change at a rate of \(R_{dyn}\) per amp.

#### A. Zener diodes

Let's work the example. We want to use a 1N4733 5.1V zener diode with a 15-20V voltage source. This
is connected just like a voltage divider, but instead of \(R_2\) we have the zener. The
maximum dynamic resistance for this diode is 7.0 ohms at 50 mA. We will make this the top end, when
we're supplying 20V. Voltage across the zener will be 5.1V, so voltage across \(R\) will be
\(20 - 5.1 = 14.9V\). Let's find a value of \(R\) that will deliver the nominal 50mA at 14.9V.

$$ R = V/I = 14.9 / 0.05 = 298 \approx 300 \Omega$$

What is the current at the low end when we're supplying 15V?

$$ I = V/R = \frac{(15 - 5.1)}{300} = 33 mA $$

We can now calculate the expected deviation at the low end of 15V by multiplying the difference
in current by the dynamic resistance.

$$ \Delta V_{out} = R_{dyn} \Delta I = 7.0 \times (0.033 - 0.05) = -0.12V $$
